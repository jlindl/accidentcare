If you're wondering what to do after a car accident, start with safety: switch on your hazard lights, turn off the engine and get everyone somewhere safe, away from traffic. Call 999 if anyone is hurt or the road is blocked. Then swap details with the other driver, take photos of the vehicles and the scene, and don't admit fault. Once everyone is safe, you can start sorting the recovery, the repairs and the claim.

That's the short version. The rest of this guide goes through each step in order, explains why it matters, and covers what to do in the days after, when the insurers start calling and your car may be off the road.

## What to do after a car accident: the first five minutes

Crashes are loud and confusing, and adrenaline makes it hard to think. Work through these in order and don't rush.

1. **Stop.** If you're involved in an accident, you must stop, even if it seems minor.
2. **Make it safe.** Hazard lights on, engine off, handbrake on. If it's safe to move, get yourself and any passengers out of the car and onto the verge or pavement, away from moving traffic. On a motorway, get behind the barrier if you can.
3. **Check for injuries.** Ask everyone involved, including the other driver and any passengers, whether they're hurt.
4. **Call 999 if anyone is hurt or the road is blocked.** Ask for the ambulance or police as needed. Don't move anyone who may be seriously injured unless they're in immediate danger.
5. **Warn other traffic.** Only if it's safe to do so. Don't put yourself at risk to place a warning triangle, and never use one on a motorway.

If nobody is hurt and the road is clear, take a breath. Nothing else needs to happen in a hurry.

## Swap details with everyone involved

You need the other driver's details to make any claim, and they need yours. Write down or photograph:

- their name, address and phone number
- the registration number of every vehicle involved
- the make, model and colour of their vehicle
- their insurance company and policy number, if they have it with them
- whether they own the vehicle, and if not, who does

Give them the same details back. If anyone refuses to give their details, or leaves the scene, note their registration and report it to the police. If you can't swap details at the scene for any reason, the Highway Code says you must report the accident to the police as soon as you can, and within 24 hours. Check the Highway Code and GOV.UK for the current rules if you're unsure.

## Take photos and note what happened

Your phone is your best tool at the roadside. Photographs taken in the moment answer questions that would otherwise turn into an argument weeks later. Try to capture:

- the position of the vehicles before they're moved, if it's safe
- the damage to every vehicle, close up and from a distance
- number plates
- the road layout, junction, signs and any road markings
- the weather, the light and the road surface
- anything that may have played a part, such as a pothole or a hidden sign

Then note the time, the exact location and what happened in your own words while it's fresh. If anyone saw the accident, ask for their name and phone number. Witnesses often leave quickly, so ask early.

Our [at-the-scene checklist](/at-the-scene) has all of this on one page, and it's worth saving on your phone before you ever need it.

## Don't admit fault at the scene

Stay calm and polite, but don't accept blame, apologise in a way that sounds like an admission, or agree to settle things privately with cash. It's natural to want to smooth things over, but at the roadside nobody has the full picture. Fault is worked out later from the details, the photos and any witness accounts.

Equally, don't argue about who caused it. Swap details, record what you can, and leave the rest for afterwards.

## What to do after the accident: the next few days

Once you're home, the practical questions start. Is the car safe to drive? Who pays for the repairs? How do you get to work? This is where many drivers end up doing a lot of chasing they don't need to.

### Get checked over if you feel unwell

Some injuries, such as whiplash, don't show up straight away. If you feel pain or stiffness in the hours or days after, contact your GP or NHS 111. Get medical help first; everything else can wait.

### Tell your insurer

Your policy will almost certainly require you to tell your own insurer about the accident, even if it wasn't your fault and you don't plan to claim on your policy. Telling them is not the same as making a claim through them.

### Think about who should handle the claim

If the accident wasn't your fault, the costs of putting it right, such as recovery, a replacement vehicle and repairs, are claimed from the at-fault driver's insurer. You don't have to manage that yourself. An accident claim management company like Accident Care can deal with the at-fault insurer and the paperwork for you. You can read more about how a [non-fault accident claim](/guides/non-fault-accident-claim) works.

### Keep everything in one place

Start a folder, paper or on your phone, for everything to do with the accident: the other driver's details, your photos, witness numbers, any letters or emails, receipts for costs such as taxis, and notes of every phone call with the date and who you spoke to.

## Common mistakes to avoid

A few things make life harder later on:

- **Leaving without the other driver's insurance details.** A name and phone number alone can be hard to follow up.
- **Not taking photos because the damage looks minor.** Damage that looks small can hide more underneath.
- **Agreeing a cash deal at the roadside.** Once you've driven away, it's very difficult to go back on.
- **Ignoring pain.** If something hurts, get it checked and keep a note of when it started.
- **Waiting too long to get help.** The sooner the claim is started, the sooner recovery, a replacement car and repairs can be arranged.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to call the police after a car accident?

Call 999 if anyone is hurt, the road is blocked or you think a crime has been committed. If you couldn't swap details at the scene, you must report the accident to the police as soon as possible, and within 24 hours. Check the Highway Code and GOV.UK for the current rules.

### Should I tell my insurer if the accident wasn't my fault?

Most policies require you to tell your insurer about any accident. Telling them isn't the same as claiming on your policy. In a non-fault claim, costs are claimed from the at-fault driver's insurer instead.

### What if the other driver won't give me their details?

Note their registration number, the make and colour of the vehicle, and anything else you can see. Take a photo if you can do so safely, and report it to the police.
